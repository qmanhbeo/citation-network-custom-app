Renewable Energy Plant Development,
Historic Environments, and Local
Communities: Lessons from the Greek
Experience
Ioannis Poulios

Hellenic Open University, Greece

The development of renewable energy plants (REPs) is mostly seen from the
technical,  economic,  and  legal  perspectives,  with  the  cultural  and  social
aspects often not being taken into account, sometimes resulting in a serious
impact  on  the  progress  of  the  investment.  This  is  particularly  evident  with
historic  environments,  where  local  communities  tend  to  have  a  negative
attitude towards the development of REPs, causing severe delays and even
the cancellation of the investment. This article examines the impact of REPs
on  historic  environments  in  relation  to  the  attitude  of  local  communities,
using  a  variety  of  case  studies  from  Greece.  The  aim  is  to  develop  a  new
approach  of  broader  applicability  that  would  link  the  development  of  REPs
to the protection of historic environments and the sustainable development
of local communities, and then provide speciﬁ c recommendations (consist-
ing  of  practical  steps)  for  the  implementation  of  this  approach  to  how  a
renewable energy project can succeed while at the same time respecting the
historic environment and engaging the local community.

keywords  renewable  energy  plant,  historic  environment,  local  community,
sustainable development, Greece

The Greek context

The development of renewable energy plants (henceforth cited as REP) is high on the
political  agenda  of  the  Greek  government,  in  an  attempt  to  comply  with  the  Euro-
pean Union (EU) regulations and also as a potential outlet from the current financial
crisis. As the former Prime Minister of Greece stated: ‘a planned long-term policy for
the promotion of the Renewable Energy Sources and for the gradual detachment from
oil and conventional fuels, in parallel with the creation of new business opportunities

The development of REPs, as with any type of urban development project, has to
ensure respect for the historic (as well as the natural) environment, and presupposes
the conduct and approval of an Environmental Impact Assessment, in the context of
‘integrated’  and  ‘holistic’  conservation.3  Respect  for  the  historic  environment  in
Greece is controlled by a strict legislative framework: the ownership of the environ-
ment  is  in  the  hands  of  the  state,  while  the  protection  of  the  environment  is  recog-
nized  as  ‘the  duty  of  the  state  and  the  right  of  everyone’.4  The  system  of  heritage
protection  is  particularly  concerned  about  ‘the  safeguarding  of  [the  monuments’]
material  substance  and  their  authenticity’5  in  an  attempt  to  ‘safeguard  the  historic
memory  for  the  sake  of  the  present  and  the  future  generations’,6  and  could  be
generally  characterized  by  an  emphasis  on  the  preservation  of  the  tangible/material
—  rather  than  the  intangible  —  elements  of  monuments.7  The  responsibility  for
protection lies with the Ministry of Culture; even in those cases where other govern-
ment  agencies  (such  as  the  Ministry  of  Environment,  Energy,  and  Climate  Change)
are  also  involved,  the  Ministry  of  Culture  retains  final  responsibility.  Protection  is
centrally  administered:  the  central  offices  of  the  Ministry  set  the  policy,  while  the
local offices deliver this policy at the local level.8

On a practical level, respect for the historic environment and, more specifically, the
conduct  and  approval  of  an  Environmental  Impact  Assessment  faces  a  number  of
difficulties.  The  criteria  that  comprise  the  ‘material  substance  and  authenticity’  (see
above)  remain  unclear,  and  guidelines  on  ways  to  define  and  safeguard  the  historic
memory  and  take  into  account  and  involve  the  present  generation,  let  alone  future
ones,  are  lacking.  There  is  no  complete  and  updated  national  archive  of  listed
monuments, and a large number of listed monuments remain unrecorded to date. The
responsibilities of the differing government bodies involved are not well defined, and
cooperation  between  the  investor  with  the  central  and  local  government  offices  in
most  cases  is  far  from  satisfactory.  Also,  there  are  no  specific  and  detailed  require-
ments for the conduct and approval of an Environmental Impact Assessment.9

Despite  this  difficult  context  for  the  development  of  REPs,  certain  investments
succeed in moving on to the stage of implementation, only to face a usually negative
attitude on the part of the local community, often resulting in considerable delays and
even the cancellation of the investment. Therefore, the development of REPs in Greece
is  placed  —  as  far  as  protection  of  the  historic  environment  is  concerned  —  under
the same legislative framework as any other type of development project: a strict and
government-controlled framework that focuses on the preservation of the material of
the  monuments.  In  practice,  however,  the  development  of  REPs  seems  to  present
further  complexities  that  may  differentiate  them  from  an  average  development
project:

for their development, tends to give a harder push on the part of the investors

officials of the Ministry of Culture) in the development of REPs — given that
REPs are a relatively new type of development — leads to vague and confusing

The operation of a wind farm near Hosios Meletios Monastery, Prefecture
of Boiotia
Significance of the historic environment (with an emphasis on the association of the

Hosios Meletios Monastery dates back to the twelfth century and is associated with
the reformation of monastic life in Greece. Today it continues to function as a living
monastery.

REP development process (with an emphasis on the attitude of the local community)
A  proposal  for  the  construction  of  a  wind  farm  in  an  area  near  the  Monastery
was  approved  by  the  Ministry  of  Culture  on  the  grounds  that  its  distance  from  the
monument was considered sufficient and thus there would be no considerable optical
interference  to  the  monument,  and  the  wind  farm  was  completed.  However,  the
monastic community argued that the noise generated by the wind turbines hindered
them  from  the  conduct  of  their  religious  rituals  and  their  everyday-life  duties,  and
called for the cessation of the operation of the wind farm. The view of the monastic
community was supported by an NGO. The Council of State decided on the cessation
of a number of the wind turbines, so that the noise generated would be decreased.11

Proposals for the construction of wind farms on Gyaros Island, Prefecture
of Cyclades
Significance of the historic environment (with an emphasis on the association of the
local community with the historic environment)
Gyaros  is  a  very  small,  arid,  and  unpopulated  island  in  the  Aegean  Sea.  The  island
was  used  as  a  place  of  exile  and  torture  for  political  dissidents  in  Greece  in  various
periods between 1948 and 1974, during the Civil War and the military junta, with the
remains  of  the  main  prison  building  and  camps  still  visible  today.  The  island  as  a
whole is recognized as a place of historic memory associated with the struggles of the
Greek  people  for  freedom  and  democracy.  The  symbolic  value  of  the  island  is  vivid
and strong, particularly for former prisoners, who have established an association for
the  safeguarding  of  the  historic  memory  of  the  place,  ‘Gyaros  Historical  Memory’,
and once a year pay tribute to their departed colleagues by holding ceremonies at the
cemetery on the island.

REP development process (with an emphasis on the attitude of the local community)
For  the  exploitation  of  the  rich  wind  energy  potential  of  the  island,  two  proposals
were  submitted  (by  two  different  firms)  for  the  construction  of  wind  farms  on  the
part  of  the  island  where  there  were  no  remaining  structures.  The  proposals  met  the
silent  consent  of  the  Municipality  of  Syros,  to  which  the  island  belongs  in  terms  of
administration, and the population of the nearby islands, but the firm opposition of
the ‘Gyaros Historical Memory’ on the basis of the potential impact of the investment
on the historic memory of the island. The issue is still in debate.12

Examples from Greece: Review

The examples cited above demonstrate that the cultural and social aspects were not
taken into account, at least sufficiently, in the planning process for the development
of  REPs,  resulting  in  a  serious  impact  on  the  progress  of  the  investments.  The  core
issue was that the planning process created a dichotomy between the historic environ-
ment  and  the  local  community,  sensing  and  treating  the  local  community  as  an
external  or  supplementary  aspect  detached  from  the  historic  environment.  This
approach  resulted  in  a  series  of  mistakes  throughout  the  process  (mistakes  that  are
present to some extent in the legislation, affect the attitude and the guidelines on the
part  of  the  government  bodies  and  their  cooperation  with  investors,  and  rule  the
practices of those investors).

First, REPs did not link the significance of the historic environment to the associa-
tion  of  the  local  community  with  the  historic  environment.  As  a  result,  the  signifi-
cance of the historic environment was mostly sensed in terms of the material/tangible
expressions  and  associated  values,  while  the  intangible  expressions  and  associated
values, to a considerable extent, were neglected. Indicative examples are as follows:

•

•

•

 in the case of St George Monastery, the religious value (through its use by the
inhabitants of the nearby villages) and the symbolic value (given its role in the
Greek Revolution) were not sufficiently considered
 in the case of Hosios Meletios Monastery, the historic value (of the monastery
structures) and the aesthetic value (avoidance of the optical interference to the
monastery)  were  emphasized,  while  the  religious  value  (through  the  practices
of the monastic community) and the symbolic value (as a place associated with
the reformation of the monastic life in Greece) were not taken into account
 with  regard  to  Gyaros  Island,  the  historic  value  (of  the  remaining  structures)
was  given  priority  over  its  symbolic  value  (through  the  associated  memories
and the rituals of the former prisoners).

Moreover, the boundaries of the historic environment in some cases were defined on
the  basis  of  material/tangible  expressions  rather  than  intangible  ones.  For  example,
the  boundaries  of  Hosios  Meletios  Monastery  were  defined  on  the  basis  of  the
monastery structures (through avoidance of the optical interference to the structures)
and not on monastic practices (through negligence of the acoustic disturbance to the
practices).  Similarly,  in  defining  the  boundaries  of  Gyaros  Island,  emphasis  was
on  the  physical  structures  (covering  a  specific  part  of  the  island),  rather  than  the
historic memory (covering the entire territory of the island).

Second,  REPs  sensed  and  approached  all  historic  environments  —  as  well  as  all
local communities — in the same way, in a ‘one size fits all’ approach, regardless of

Third, REPs did not see the historic environment, through its association with its

in  some  cases  appeared  to  focus  on  its  current/present  phase  (the  one  ‘hosting’  the

main  construction  phase,  rather  than  the  pre-  and  especially  the  post-construction
phase (i.e. the aftermath of the investment). The omission to carry out a pre-construction
phase  was  illustrated  in  the  case  of  Naxos  Island,  where  no  provision  was  made
for  transferring  the  elements  of  the  wind  turbines  through  the  inhabited  landscape.
Failure  in  the  post-construction  phase  was  evident  in  the  case  of  Hosios  Meletios
Monastery where no provision was made for the impact of the operation of the wind
turbines  on  the  practices  of  the  monastic  community,  and  also  in  the  case  of  the
Acheloos  River  Diversion  Scheme,  whose  construction  would  have  led  to  radical
changes in the broader landscape and the economy and life of the local community.
Fourth,  REPs  did  not  involve  the  local  community  in  the  process.  There  was
insufficient  awareness  of  the  characteristics  of  the  local  community  —  in  terms
of structure, management systems, processes and people, and concerns, — and there
was  no  provision  for  the  establishment  of  a  formula  of  cooperation  with  the  local
community. The local community was taken into account only at a very late stage in
the  process,  when  it  could  not  have  been  possibly  avoided.  At  that  stage,  the  local
community  retained  a  negative  attitude  towards  the  development  of  REP,  causing
severe delays and even the cancellation of the investments. The attitude of negligence
towards the local community and the consequences on the progress of the investment
were  evident  in  the  cases  of  St  George  Monastery,  Hosios  Meletios  Monastery,  and
Gyaros Island.

Towards a new approach (of broader applicability)

A new approach is required for the development of REPs to link them to the protec-
tion  of  the  historic  environment  and  the  sustainable  development  of  the  local
community.  This  new  approach  is  based  on  the  notion  that  the  local  community  is
treated  as  an  inseparable  part  of  the  historic  environment,  and  follows  certain  key
principles.

First, REPs must link the significance of the historic environment to the association
of  the  local  community  with  the  environment.  An  historic  environment,  defined
through its association with the local community, consists of the following elements:15

a)   The  function  of  the  historic  environment.  Indicative  examples:  religious

 The  values  inscribed  to  the  historic  environment  by  the  community.  Values
include those associated to the material expressions, such as historic, aesthetic,
scientific/research,  and  economic  ones,  and  those  associated  to  the  intangible
expressions,  such  as  social,  religious,  and  symbolic  ones,16  which  should  be
given equal attention.

c)

d)   Community’s  care  of  the  historic  environment,  expressed  through  the  owner-
ship  status,  the  management  systems,  and  the  maintenance  practices.  These
can be traditional ones (such as customary law, sacred and pragmatic controls,
and  traditions:17  for  example,  the  Abbot  and  the  Council  of  Hosios  Meletios
Monastery) or modern, scientific-based ones (such as modern legislation).

Second,  REPs  see  each  historic  environment,  through  its  association  with  its  com-
munity, as a unique one, and treat them differently. Several types of historic environ-
ment may be identified, based on different types of community association with the
environments, each association being of different strength. These different types have
to do with the differing ways in which the community’s association with the historic
environment  has  evolved  over  time  to  date,  in  terms  of  function,  material/tangible
and  intangible  expressions,  values,  and  care  (see  above).  Specifically,  the  following
types  of  historic  environment  may  be  identified  (from  the  weakest  to  the  strongest
association of communities with the environment):18

•

 An  historic  environment  with  a  (simply)  local  community  —  i.e.  any  commu-
nity  living  in  or  near  a  historic  environment  —  whose  association  with  the
environment is a relatively weak one. In the example of Gyaros Island (though,
strictly  speaking,  there  is  no  ‘local’  community  in  the  sense  that  the  island  is
uninhabited)  the  inhabitants  of  the  nearby  islands,  which  is  the  community
with  the  closest  geographical  proximity  to  the  island,  have  a  very  weak  asso-

consent to the investment plans.
 An  historic  environment  with  a  dwelling  community,  whose  association  with
the  environment  might  still  not  be  a  particularly  strong  one.  This  could  be
the case of Naxos Island, judged by the absence of an active reaction from the
local  inhabitants  to  the  destruction  caused  to  the  historic  environment  by  the

 An  historic  environment  with  a  changing/evolving  (not  the  original)  commu-

 An  historic  environment  whose  community  has  a  claimed  special  association

a tribute to their departed colleagues, as well as through their firm opposition
to the investment plans.

Third, REPs see the historic environment, through its association with its local com-
munity  (in  terms  of  function,  material/tangible  and  intangible  expressions,  values,
and care; see above), as being in continuous evolution over time and into the future,
and  attempt  to  associate  themselves  with  this  evolution.  The  current/present  phase
of  a  historic  environment  is  not  seen  as  the  final  one,  and  thus  the  emphasis  is  not
only  on  the  REP’s  main  construction  phase  but  also  on  the  pre-  and  especially  the
post-construction phase (i.e. the aftermath of the investment).

Fourth, REPs involve the local community in the process from the very beginning,
getting  to  know  the  characteristics  of  the  community  and  establishing  a  formula  of
cooperation with it. Therefore, REPs consider each historic environment, as defined
through  its  association  with  its  local  community,  to  be  unique  and  in  continuous
evolution over time to date and into the future, and accordingly approach and involve
the local community from the very beginning.

The new approach, as outlined above, could be implemented through the following
recommendations (consisting of practical steps) on how an REP project can succeed,
while  at  the  same  time  respecting  the  historic  environment  and  engaging  the  local
community:

Concluding remarks

Cultural  and  social  aspects  are  of  crucial  importance  for  the  development  of  REPs,
particularly  in  the  historic  environment.  Therefore,  a  new  approach  is  suggested
which  links  the  development  of  REPs  to  the  protection  of  the  historic  environment
and  the  sustainable  development  of  the  local  community.  This  strategy  attempts  to
point  towards  a  different  way  of  understanding,  planning,  and  implementing  the
development of REPs. A REP should not be seen only as an external intervention to
a historic environment, with — almost by definition — a rather negative impact on
it; rather, the focus should be on how to limit this impact for the sake of the protec-
tion of the historic environment. A REP may now be seen also as part of the historic
environment, in accordance with the specific characteristics of it (as defined through
its association with the local community), as another step — not the final one — in
the evolution of the historic environment over time and into the future. Accordingly,
our focus should be on how to manage, support, and guide this evolution.
