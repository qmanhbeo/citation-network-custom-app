Just transition: A conceptual review

Xinxin Wang, Kevin Lo *

Department of Geography, Hong Kong Baptist University, Hong Kong, China

A R T I C L E  I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Just transition
Energy transition
Low-carbon transition
Conceptual review

The growing attention paid to the idea of a just transition away from the incumbent fossil fuel energy paradigm
has led scholars to devise diverse definitions, understandings, and viewpoints of the term. This review seeks to
clarify the different perspectives surrounding the concept, to consolidate knowledge, and to provide a concise
account of current debates in the literature as well as a research agenda. It identifies five themes around which
the concept has been discussed: (1) just transition as a labor-oriented concept, (2) just transition as an integrated
framework  for  justice,  (3)  just  transition  as  a  theory  of  socio-technical  transition,  (4)  just  transition  as  a
governance strategy, and (5) just transition as public perception. Overall, this review suggests that the literature
on just transition employs rich theoretical and empirical insights from various disciplines yet contains several
gaps. Specifically, it argues that the literature would benefit from more empirical studies rooted in practice, more
discussion on the relationship between different concepts of just transition, an expansion of geographical scope to
include  developing  countries  and  non-democratic  regimes,  and  more  attention  to  power  dynamics  in  just
transition.

1. Introduction

Addressing  the  threat  of  anthropogenic  climate  change  requires  a
fundamental transition from the incumbent fossil fuel energy system to a
low/zero carbon system [1–3]. Rather than completely shifting from one
set of dominant energy resources to another, energy transition involves
the  phasing  out  of  fossil  fuels, while  increasing  renewable  energy  re-
sources such as solar and wind, which used to occupy only a small share
of  the  energy  mix  [4].  This  shift  necessitates  complicated  social  and
technological changes and will have a profound social impact. Scholars
have indicated that such a shift must consciously avoid reproducing the
environmental and socio-economic injustices and inequalities which are
intrinsic  to  the  existing  energy  regime  [4–8].  Indeed,  the  low-carbon
transition  is  a  multifaceted  phenomenon  entailing  considerable  bene-
fits and risks [9]. The benefits of shifting to a less carbon-intensive socio-
technical regime include more resilient and democratic energy systems
[10],  industrial  restructuring  and  technological  advancements  [11],
poverty alleviation [12], and addressing social and environmental jus-
tice  concerns  within  and  across  nations  [13].  The  development  of
renewable energy is considered a crucial tool for advancing sustainable
development goals, as it could replace the highly polluted, dangerous,
grey economy with an environmentally friendly, healthy, green econ-
omy [13].

The costs and risks of such a fundamental transition, however, are
troubling.  The decline  of carbon-intensive industries has caused diffi-
culties for individuals who held fossil fuel jobs. They often encounter
difficulties in finding a new job because of a skills gap or are forced to
make  sacrifices  such  as  substantial  wage  loss  and  long-distance  com-
mutes,  along  with  compromises  in  culture,  community  identity,  and
sense of place [4,14]. Moreover, fossil fuel industries play an important
role  in  the  local  tax  revenue  base;  the  decline  of  the  industry  may
adversely affect public services such as education, transportation, and
waste management [4]. Furthermore, the social consequences of labor
disruptions extend to other employment sectors within fossil fuel-based
communities [4].

Justice concerns in the low-carbon transitions arise not only from the
fossil fuel industry decline, but also from the inequitable distribution of
benefits.  Studies  indicate  that  renewable  energy,  the  so-called  “green
jobs creators,” may hardly benefit marginalized groups. The represen-
tation of women and people of color in the United States solar industry
workforce is well below the national average for all occupations [4]. In
Germany,  the  development  of  solar  energy  shares  many  of  the  same
inequalities that characterize fossil fuel industry, resulting in the crea-
tion of localized “sacrifice zones” [15]. On a global scale, the trend of
male dominance in highly technical, well-paid jobs remains a constant in
the  renewable  energy  sector  [4].  Strikingly,  the  disproportion

Therefore, the low-carbon transition is certainly not a magic elixir for
a  more  “just”  world  despite  its  environmental  and  economic  benefits
[20]. As Sovacool et al. [9] rightly point out, the current wave of low-
carbon  transitions  may  “create  new  injustices  and  vulnerabilities,
while also failing to address pre-existing structural drivers of injustice in
energy markets and the wider socio-economy.” It is therefore imperative
to shift the technology-focused understanding of low-carbon transition
to include greater engagement with social justice issues [21]. As Wil-
liams and Doyon argue, “we cannot achieve a sustainability transition
without justice, indeed that an unjust transition is not sustainable” [22].
In this context, the concept of just transition, which draws attention to
the equity and justice issues associated with efforts to address energy
and  climate  problems,  is  increasingly  recognized  as  an  important
component of low-carbon transition regimes [23–27].

The  concept  of  a  just  transition  has  a  long  history  and  has  been
applied by activists, labor unions, and associated groups dating back to
the early 1970s [23,25], while scholarly debates on transition appeared
more  recently  in  the  2000s  [28].  Yet,  in  a  relatively  short  time,  this
concept has spawned an expansive body of literature, and scholars have
committed  significant  efforts  to  the  conceptual  development  of  just
transition [8,24,29]. In particular, multidisciplinary engagement with
the concept has led to divergent interpretations, resulting in multiple
and ambiguous definitions [27,30,31]. There is no universally agreed-
upon definition of or framework for just transitions [31]. The concept
of just transition has become, in our view, so ambiguous and with so
many different meanings that communication and debate have become
difficult. An overview and consolidation of academic literature around
just transition is urgently needed to clarify these different conceptions
and  their  inter-relationships.  Another  motivation  of  this  study  is  to
identify  the  limitations  of  existing  research  in  just  transition  and  to
suggest a research agenda.

A few reviews engage with the concept of just transition, but they are
often limited to one particular strand of the literature. For instance, in
their conceptual review, García-García et al. [32] review the just tran-
sition literature with specific attention on the impact of the low-carbon
transition on labor income. Jenkins et al. [33] focus on the applications
of the energy justice framework in low-carbon transition studies. This
approach is echoed by Sovacool and Dworkin [34], who develop energy
justice as a conceptual tool, analytical tool, and decision-making tool for
three groups of people: philosophers and ethicists; energy researchers;
energy  planners  and  consumers.  From  a  climate  justice  perspective,
Sonja and Hadi [35] provide a conceptual review with a specific focus on
distributive justice implementation and its potential outcomes in inter-
national climate policies. Because the existing reviews mainly focus on
one interpretation, they do not reflect the diversities of just transition
and the relationship among the different dimensions of just transition.
This paper provides a comprehensive conceptual review of the just
transition literature. The method of review is based on the systematic
review approach typically adopted in the field of energy studies [32].
First, to determine the objective of the study, an analysis of the most
referenced articles, particularly review papers, was conducted. This led
to the realization that a review of just transitions to clarify the different
interpretations was needed. Second, we employed an inclusive search
strategy  to  retrieve  papers  containing  the  different  conceptual  in-
terpretations. Keywords adopted were not restricted to “just transition,”
but also related concepts including “justice,”  “equity,”  “fairness,”  and
“ethics” in conjunction with “energy / low-carbon / clean / sustainable /
green  transition.”  These  keywords  were  used  to  search  databases
including Web of Science and Google Scholar. We then searched addi-
tional references by checking the bibliography of the papers. Third, the
publications  collected  were  checked  to  ensure  their  relevance  and
analyzed through an inductive approach to thematic analysis. Then, a
qualitative synthesis of the themes was conducted. Our search was not

taken up by climate justice activists in recent decades [25,31]. Together,
these movements contributed to the acknowledgement of a need for a
just transition within the final agreement of the 2010 United Nations
Climate  Change  Conference  [24].  Today,  the  scope  of  just  transition
discourse encompasses a range of issues, including food justice, green
gentrification, and energy sovereignty [31].

The concept of a labor-oriented just transition is increasingly inter-
twined with calls for fossil fuel-dependent countries to develop future-
oriented climate policies and economic development plans [23,25,31].
These  calls  seek  to  garner  broad  public  support,  break  the  fossil  fuel
industry’s  stranglehold  on  energy  policies,  accelerate  the  pace  of  a
transition away from an extractive economy to a regenerative one, and
ultimately  mitigate  the  severe  economic  hardships  that  fossil  fuel-
dependent  communities  and  workers  will  face  in  the  future  [36,37].
Policies  encouraging  the  growth  of  renewable  energy  have  become  a
predominant remediation means for workers and communities affected
by  declining  fossil  fuel  industries.  However,  the  development  of
renewable energy may be contested in regions where the development
are meant to aid, as fossil fuel-based communities and workers may feel
threatened by the societal shift toward clean energy [36]. Furthermore,
the definition of a just transition is often contested in practice, especially
in  the  context  of  multi-actor  policy  networks.  While  labor  unions
advocate  for  finding  or  creating  decent,  well-paying  jobs  that  are
equivalent to the jobs that fossil fuel workers and others in their com-
munities have lost, environmental groups emphasize the need for sus-
tainable  ecological  outcomes  [27].  However,  a  study  exploring  the
transition away from coal production in Australia’s Latrobe Valley—a
heartland  region  that  generates  85–90%  of  the  country’s  elec-
tricity—following the shutdown of the Hazelwood power station found
that,  despite  inconsistent  definitions  of  what  a  just  transition  entails,
invoking the concept “has served to open up a meaningful discussion
between unions, environmentalists, and others on how best to mitigate
the  social  fallout  from  necessary  environmental  policy  changes  and
generate jobs” [27].

Advocates for a just transition do not see a conflict between envi-
ronmental protection and economic vitality because they suggest that
more  jobs  could  be  created  in  an  environmentally  friendly  economy
[24]. These advocates aim to foster an alternative socio-environmental
politics  which  values  collective  representation  by  workers  and  com-
munities  around  principles  of  social  and  environmental  justice  [28].
More  recently,  Henry  et  al.  suggest  that  the  ongoing  COVID-19
pandemic might represent a catalyst for community and economic re-
covery efforts that support “a shift to a low-carbon energy system that
prioritizes  sustainability  and  social  and  economic  equity”  [31].  The
optimistic attitude is also echoed by White, who points out that as the
understanding  of  the  labor-based  concept  increasingly  has  been
expanded  by  labor,  socialist,  climate  justice,  environmental  justice,
feminist,  and  decolonial  factions,  this  expansion  could  contribute  to
enrich the meaning of justice and to facilitate just transition alliances in
low-carbon transitions [38]. Based on this observation, White proposes
using  the  concept  of  just  transition  not  only  to  enhance  coalition-
building capacity and to mobilize greater engagement of a range of so-
cial justice advocacy organizations, but also as a useful tool for public
dialogues, allowing a very different kind of technical, cultural, political
and economic knowledge to be integrated into the transition discursive
formations  [38].  However,  opposing this  optimistic  view, Kalt  argues
that just transition as a worker-centered concept would lead to a slow-
down  of  low-carbon  transition  [39].  In  examining  the  historical
impact  of  labor  and  climate  movements  on  the  jobs  versus  climate
conflicts, Kalt points out that just transition has become a compromising
outcome as climate movements failed in attracting labor union support,
and labor unions remain allied with the fossil fuel industry to protect
high-paying industrial jobs [39].

Studies adopting the labor-oriented approach typically focus on the
agency of national and global labor unions [28]. However, while labor
unions have indeed played a vital role in advocating for a just transition,
they should not be perceived as a homogenous entity; rather, there are
apparent  tensions  within  and  between  trade  unions  about  which  and
whose jobs they should be protecting and at what cost. Furthermore,
business groups often mobilize labor unions to oppose ambitious climate
action or support carbon intensive infrastructures (such as airports), and
the role of government as a powerful actor regulating the relationship
between businesses and labor unions cannot be understated. Therefore,
it  is  also  important  to  explore  under  what  conditions  and  by  what
strategies these stakeholders engage in the politics of just transition and
with what results.

3. Just transition as an integrated framework for justice

In the second strand of the literature, scholars attempt to transcend
the  historical  meaning  of  a  just  transition  described  above  by  recog-
nizing  the  linkage  between  the  concept  with  other,  more  established
concepts  in  the  justice-related  literature,  including  environmental,
climate, and energy justice. We discuss each of the three concepts in turn
to highlight these integrative attempts.

3.1. Environmental justice

The concept of environmental justice is historically grounded in the
environmental justice movement, which has a long history of being at
the forefront of fighting for vulnerable groups that are disproportion-
ately  impacted  by  environmental degradation [40–42].  Advocates  for
environmental justice argue that disadvantaged communities should not
shoulder a disproportionate burden when it comes to the damage caused
by environmental issues [22], and all citizens should be equally involved
in the development, implementation, and enforcement of environmen-
tally  just  policies  [43].  Schlosberg  and  Collins  note  that  the  environ-
mental justice movement has a broad agenda that links environmental
and other social justice concerns such as the occupational health and
safety  movement,  the  indigenous  land  rights  movement,  the  public
health movement, and various social and economic justice movements,
through  building  alliances  with  labor  unions,  environmentalists,  and
civil  rights  groups  [19].  Additionally,  environmental  justice  activists
bring a wide variety of experiences, that in turn enrich the theoretical
development of justice [19,44–46].

With the recognition that the causes of environmental pollution are
rooted  in  political,  economic,  and  cultural  inequalities,  the  environ-
mental  justice  concept  takes  a  more  holistic  view  to  identify  who  is
affected, and in what ways and how to address those inequities [14,40].
Consequently, in addition to its initial focus on the inequitable distri-
bution of environmental risks, the elements of recognition and partici-
pation  have  been  integrated  into  environmental  justice  advocacy  as
crucial demands [19,42]. Scholars highlight that recognition justice is
an  inherent  precondition  for  distribution  justice  because  without
knowing who is affected, and in what ways, a fair distribution of benefits
and harms cannot happen [42]. As Fraser [47] claims, “only by looking
to alternative conceptions of redistribution and recognition can we meet
the requirements of justice for all.” In addition, recognition is an integral
part of procedural justice because no one can participate without being
recognized  and  respected  [42].  In  turn,  democratic  and  participatory
decision-making procedures could provide solutions to address inequi-
table  distribution  and  ensure  substantive  participation  for  affected
vulnerable groups [42]. Overall, distribution justice, recognition justice
and  procedural  justice  are  seen  as  interlinked  and  inseparable
[42,48,49].

Given that the environmental justice framework has been expanding
topically and geographically over decades, it has a large influence on the
development of other justice-related concepts, including climate justice
[19,50]  and  energy  justice  [21,51,52].  For  instance,  Schlosberg  and

It  is  also  evident  that  the  concept  of  environmental  justice  has
become closely related to just transition, both theoretically and practi-
cally  [56].  Historically,  the  environmental  justice  concept  has  been
criticized  as  focusing  too  much  on  defending  against  environmental
threats, whereas less emphasis has been placed on socioeconomic issues
like job security and economic equity. This leads to a lack of capacity to
engage  with  fossil  fuel  dependent  communities,  workers  and  their
unions, thereby weakening the ability to unsettle the hegemony of the
fossil fuel industries [57]. These challenges have led Evans and Phelan to
argue  that  the  environmental  justice  communities  “simply  advocate
shifting hazardous industries from one locality to another,” and fails to
ease  the  perceived  tension  between  economic  vitality  and  environ-
mental  protection  as  a  result.  Recently,  scholars  and  practitioners  of
environmental  justice  have  expanded  their  work  to  not  merely
responding to immediate environmental hazards, but to include future-
oriented approaches to socio-economic justice and sustainability, such
as shifting from an extractive to a regenerative economy. For example,
the  Just  Transition  Alliance  in  the  United  States  brought  together
numerous  environmental  justice  organizations  to  conceptualize  and
frame  a  more  integrated  approach  to  just  transitions.  Likewise,  local
organizations  in  coal-producing  regions  such  as  Wyoming  and  New
Mexico  emphasize  labor-focused  just  transition  frameworks  often  use
environmental justice principles in their advocacy, such as a focus on
“more participatory, deliberative, non-hierarchical sets of relations be-
tween  states,  citizens  and  communities”  [58].  As  such,  both  environ-
mental justice and just transition frameworks now encompass, but also
extend beyond the concerns of fossil fuel producing communities, such
as including people who have been living with higher levels of pollution
because  of  the  disproportionate  location  of  fossil  fuel  facilities  in
marginalized communities [14]. This conceptual development suggests
that weaving together environmental justice and just transition could
attract the support of those who may be vulnerable and marginalized in
a post-carbon economy and pose a powerful and united front to the fossil
fuel bloc [57].

3.2. Climate justice

The concept of climate justice has evolved in step with the concept of
environmental  justice,  as  they  both  share  activist  roots  [22].  The
prominence of the climate justice movement is reflected in the fact that
it brings a large and diverse array of groups together that seek to chal-
lenge  the  systems  that  create  climate  change  and  impose  dispropor-
tionate  environmental  costs  and  risks  on  less  powerful  groups  [59].
Harris claims that climate change is “a matter of justice” [60], and he
defines climate justice as a broader understanding of justice in terms of
the “fairness, equity and rightness” of responses toward climate change
issues [60]. Klinsky et al. summarize the four reasons that justice and
equity are centered in climate change:

Obligation to address human wellbeing…; Understandings of justice
are essential to political analysis…; Equity is not always in tension
with  strong  climate  action  or  collective  action…;  Understanding
trade-offs requires taking equity into account [61].

Climate justice is used routinely to frame discussions about historical
responsibility, finance, loss and damage, human rights, and adaptation
[62–65].  Advocates  for  climate  justice  are  not  only  concerned  with
environmental issues but also acknowledge that climate crises exacer-
bate existing social inequalities and, therefore, advocate for policies that
mitigate the effects of climate change and adapt society to the emergent
reality  of  climate  change  over  the  long  term.  Okereke  highlights  the
three key elements of climate justice as follows:

Compensation for past harm (compensatory justice), distribution of
future and present responsibilities (distributive justice), and adop-
tion  of  fair  procedures  and  inclusive  framework  in  the  process  of
reaching decision making (procedural justice) [66].

Many of these advocates perceive climate justice in the context of the
uneven distribution of global warming’s effects on developing countries.
They argue that countries that “contributed the most to the occurrence
of the situation are more prepared”  to deal with its effects than those
that did not [26], and that impoverished people in developing nations
who are least responsible for the climate crisis will suffer more from its
effects than individuals in wealthy nations who lead high-carbon life-
styles [28]. They also argue that the people who will be most affected by
climate change participate the least in the global decision-making pro-
cess regarding climate change, which raises issues of procedural injus-
tice [26]. Moreover, intergenerational justice is one of the key ethical
concerns  of  climate  justice  because  the  future  generation  will  be  the
most affected by the climate catastrophe but excluded from the relevant
policy-making process [66].

Various  climate  justice  organizations,  such  as  the  Climate  Justice
Alliance,  Climate  Justice  Now,  and  Movement  Generation,  have
contributed to the integration between climate justice and just transi-
tion. However, many countries are reluctant to take positive action to
limit global warming. In this context, Routledge et al. (2018) point out
that  climate  justice  activists  could  learn  lessons  from  just  transition
movements insofar as the latter’s emphasis on grassroots organizing can
cultivate “more participatory, deliberative, non-hierarchical sets of re-
lations between  states, citizens and  communities”  and open  up  possi-
bilities for just and sustainable futures [58].

3.3. Energy justice

Tackling climate change requires drastic changes in energy produc-
tion and consumption. However, the distribution of harm and benefits
within the incumbent energy system are unequal; thus, there is growing
recognition in the literature that a just transition to a low-carbon future
requires just trade-offs between “energy and development and carbon
growth” so that all nations can benefit equally from the transition [23].
Theorizing these trade-offs is the domain of energy justice—the pursuit
of “a global energy system that fairly distributes both the benefits and
burdens of energy services, and one that contributes to more represen-
tative  and  inclusive  energy  decision-making”  [67].  Energy  justice
scholars attempt to develop a framework for energy justice that can be
applied in practice [63]. Such a framework evaluates “where injustices
emerge, who is affected or ignored, and what processes exist for reme-
diation  to  reveal  and  reduce  injustices”  [22].  Such  frameworks  are
undergirded  by  four  tenets:  procedural  justice,  distributional  justice,
justice as recognition, and restorative justice [68]. The energy justice
literature  covers  various  energy-related  issues,  including  energy
poverty, large-scale energy projects, and energy transition [54,69–71].

3.4. Just transition as an integrated framework

Each of these three justice frameworks (environment, climate, and
energy) emphasize distributive justice and procedural justice and agree
that despite the urgent need for a transition, such a transition must be
just and equal in its effects and implementation [56]. However, it has
been argued that because each of these frameworks treats their concepts
as separate constructs, a joint conceptual space for reflection is absent
[56].  Therefore,  scholars  propose  developing  a  new  analytical  frame-
work,  which  unites  these  three  perspectives  with  the  labor-oriented
justice  concept.  Different  approaches  have  been  taken.  For  example,
some scholars suggest creating a multidisciplinary justice-based frame-
work [6], and others propose combining all three perspectives on justice
under a just transition framework [43,56]. Still others suggest a more
practical focus from the perspective of legal geography; one that em-
phasizes taking legal action to reduce inequality and injustice during the
period  of  energy  transition  [2].  Williams  and  Doyon  suggest  that  ap-
proaches to transition, environmental justice, and energy justice have
not adequately explored recognition-based justice, and thus produce an
analytical  framework  for  assessing  “issues  of  power”  in  this  domain
[22].

The concept of just transition thus offers an open space for devel-
oping an interdisciplinary approach to generate justice-oriented social
formations  in  the  context  of  a  changing  climate  [56].  Combining  the
fundamental  principles  of  justice—distributional  justice,  procedural
justice, justice as recognition, and restorative justice—is key to devel-
oping a robust framework through which to analyze the possibilities for

a just transition. However, these frameworks are primarily normative
and theoretical, and there is a lack of research that explores the prac-
ticality  and  challenges  of  applying  the  proposed  frameworks  in  real
situations.

4. Just transition as a theory of socio-technical transition

Socio-technical  transitions  refer  to  deep  structural  changes  in  sys-
tems that involve long-term and complex reconfigurations of technolo-
gies, policy, infrastructure, scientific knowledge, and social and cultural
practices [23]. Theories of socio-technical transition seek to understand
the trajectory of technological change and to reveal the origins, patterns,
and mechanisms that drive them [22]. The concept of socio-technical
transition emerged in literature in the 1990s. It suggests that techno-
logical  transitions  are  more  likely  to  occur  alongside  radical  shifts  in
society’s  structure,  values,  and  use  of  technology;  thus,  the  socio-
technical  transition  framework  explicitly  engage  with  the  complexity
and uncertainty of transitions and that they might lead to fundamental
changes in life and society [74].

The multi-level perspective is an influential analytical framework for
understanding  the  multi-dimensional  complexity  of  socio-technical
transitions [75]. The multi-level perspective conceptualizes transitions
as dynamic processes driven by three analytical levels: sociotechnical
regimes, niche-innovations, and sociotechnical landscape [76,77]. The
three  analytical  levels  are  summarized  by  Geels  et  al.  as  the  socio-
technical system (i.e., the dominant existing system), niche innovations,
which  differ  radically  from  the  existing  system,  and  exogenous  de-
velopments that destabilize the system and facilitate the breakthrough
of niche innovations [77].

Geels et al. also point out that the multi-level perspective does not
pay sufficient attention to the distribution of social benefits and burdens
or to social sustainability issues such as inequality, poverty, and work
conditions  [78].  The  approach’s  focus  on  the  dynamic  processes  of
transitions means that social outcomes or impacts of such transitions are
ignored, as transition scholars typically take it for granted that “green
innovations are intrinsically positive” [78].

Transition management is another commonly adopted approach to
study  socio-technical  transitions.  The  framework  is  a  reflexive  and
participatory governance concept that seeks to manage changes in the
desired direction by combining long-term thinking with short-term ac-
tion through a way of learning by doing and doing by learning [74,79].
There are many advantages to adopting this approach including keeping
track of  the  transition  process to  avoid  undesirable  deviations and  to
ensure  the  coalition  and  engagement  of  relevant  stakeholders  [33].
However, some scholars assert that this specific approach is difficult to
apply  in  practice  and  the  effectiveness  of  this  approach  cannot  be
assessed [33].

These analytical frameworks have been applied to a variety of issues
in the energy transition literature, such as sustainable development and
low-carbon energy transitions [76,80–86]. However, these studies have
been criticized for their narrow focus on transition management, which
is  inconsistent  with  the  disruptive  and  systemic-structural  nature  of
socio-technical  transformations  [7].  Because  they  entail  systemic
change, energy transitions inevitably bring the danger of unequal dis-
tribution of benefits and losses. Thus, some scholars have suggested that
considering this unequal distribution is a key component of “ensuring
that system transitions are not only more sustainable, but also more just”
[22].  Because  the  transition  literature  does  not  necessarily  address
“equity, justice and the political economy of transitions,” scholars have
called for increased focus on these efforts [5,22].

These calls have led to the emergence of “just energy transition,” a
discourse that centers social justice in energy transition to respond to the
dual challenges of climate change and sustainability [87]. For example,
García-García et al. view just energy transition as a renewed vision of
just transition [32]. They define a just energy transition towards a low-
carbon economy as:

…a long-term technological and socio-economic process of structural
shift that affects the generation, distribution, storage and use of en-
ergy and causes rearrangements at micro (innovation), meso (social

Thus,  the  global  low-carbon  transition  is  such  an  extraordinarily
tangled and complicated phenomenon that not only the sociotechnical
matters—the  profound  structural  change  requires  technology  and  in-
novations, while social justice aspects are also critically at stake in such
transformations [49]. While the two dimensions, socio-technical tran-
sition  and  social  justice  implications,  have  been  treated  as  distinct
scholarship in the past, some scholars have recently attempted to bridge
this gap. For example, Jenkins et al. [88] propose a hegemonic frame-
work  to  combine  justice  principles  with  socio-technical  transition
studies.  By  arguing  that  justice  concerns  would  be  difficult  to  fully
consider among other complex social contexts through the multi-level
perspective  framework,  Sareen  and  Haarstad  [49]  propose  an  inclu-
sive approach to synthesize the strengths of social justice research and
social-technical literature, instead of developing a new framework by
combining energy justice with socio-technical transition theories.

Other scholars have addressed the question of how to achieve a just
energy transition in recent years. Heffron et al. highlight that industrial
demand  could  help  attain  a  just  energy  transition  given  industry’s
flexible potential “to address short-run and unexpected imbalances be-
tween demand and supply”  [1]. Kelly et al. provide an empirical dis-
cussion  of  just  energy  transition  in  their  study  of  energy  poverty
mitigation  in  Ireland  [89].  In  short,  socio-technical  transition  frame-
works continue to be critical to the wider scholarly inquiry on energy
transition  as  the  literature  recognizes  that  profound  energy  shifts
involve multi-dimensional struggles in incumbent social, technical, and
technological systems [78]. However, given the complex magnitude and
numerous challenges of such transitions, the interdisciplinary engage-
ment at this point is far from sufficient.

5. Just transition as governance strategies

The  concept  of  governance  is  considered  to  have  created  the
groundwork for transition studies and continues to maintain its popu-
larity  within  the  field.  Moss  suggests  that  governance  approaches  to
transition  studies  are  useful  because  they  can  capture  “the  growing
complexity  of  institutional  structures,  political  processes,  and  social
relations involved in the collective pursuit of public, common, or indi-
vidual interests” [90]. In a similar vein, Huang and Liu define transition
governance as a “multi-dimensional, multi-level, multi-actor, and multi-
phase governing process to facilitate systemic transformations of socio-
technical regimes towards sustainability” [87].

Although  governance  in  the  name  of  sustainable  development  at-
tempts to steer society toward a more sustainable future, we cannot take
for  granted  that  sustainability-oriented  policies  will  produce  just  and
equal results [90]. The fundamental conditions, namely high emissions,
poverty  and  inequality,  pose  crucial  governance  challenges  [8],
including  simultaneously  addressing  the  politics  of  transition  and
ensuring a socially just transition [23]. Swilling et al. state that “a just
transition is only possible if the overall goal is human wellbeing within a
sustainable world” [91]. Similarly, Hopwood, Mellor and O’Brien [92]
argue that sustainable development is about linking human equity to the
environment. The principle of equity should be explicitly expressed in
relevant policies [40]. This view is also echoed by other scholars [38,61]
who argue that a clear equity language is not only important for justice
advocates in the stage of policy design, but could ensure that the prin-
ciple of equity is implemented as intended [40]. For Muttitt and Kartha,
the  important  policy  principles  of  just  transition  are  economic

efficiency,  meeting  development  needs,  and  effort-sharing  by  a  fully
supportive government [93]. In particular, the authors call for wealthier
regions or countries to share burdens of the poorer nations so that the
low-carbon  transitions  can  be  moved  forward  in  a  more  just  manner
[93].

Turning  to  the  governance  approach  of  just  transition,  scholars
typically attribute a successful just transition to support from a broad
coalition of actors and to engagement with local initiatives. Cha et al.
suggest that a just transition could be achieved through four governance
strategies:  strong  governmental  support,  dedicated  funding  streams,
diverse and strong coalitions, and economic diversification [14]. Newell
and  Mulvaney  suggest  that  such  coalitions  could  be  built  through
engagement with social movements and community actors to develop an
alternative vision and bring together a coalition of actors to provide and
finance ‘just energy’ [23]. Such an approach is essential to overcome the
difficult political tradeoffs that characterize a just transition. This view is
echoed  by  Winkler,  who  also  stresses  that  coalition  is  a  foundational
element  for  a  just  transition  movement,  which  refers  to  creating  alli-
ances that include a range of stakeholders, such as labor unions, social
movements, non-state actors, and some firms and international organi-
zations [8]. The author further suggests that just transition as a broad
concept allows different actors to make their own interpretations, which
is not an obstacle if all support the objectives of a just transition.

Thus, the conceptualization of just transition governance is primarily
conducted  in  the  context  of  democratic  regimes,  where  the  role  of
network and participatory governance is heavily emphasized. Healy and
Barry  suggest  that  just  transition  should  be  considered  as  a  form  of
“energy democracy” [7]. A strong coalition consists of a wide range of
actors  to  reduce  friction  between  various  stakeholders  with  different
interests  and  to  allow  for  open  and  democratic  participation  by  nar-
rowing exclusion and inequality and permitting all voices to be heard
[9]. This does not mean that just transitions happen in democratic re-
gimes automatically, but to some extent, democracies do have institu-
tional advantages to achieving a just transition owing to their political
system [9].

Modes of governance, however, differ significantly across different
socio-political contexts. Although governments can choose any combi-
nation  of  governance  approaches,  each  government’s  style  of  gover-
nance is rooted in the existing political system [87]. The key question,
thus, is how just transition as a democratic-framed governance concept
could be applied in a non-democratic context. To answer this question, a
small but growing body of literature has begun the exploration of a just
transition approach in authoritarian regimes. Unlike the energy transi-
tion  processes  in  liberal  democracies  characterized  by  municipal
voluntarism,  participation,  and  gradualness,  the  authoritarian  gover-
nance model adopts a top-down manner that stresses policy efficiency,
while leaving little space for participatory decision-making and context-
based policy adjustments [87]. Furthermore, tight control of the media,
non-governmental organizations, and academia, means that these non-
state  actors  have  limited  freedom  to  raise  social  justice  concerns.  As
such, disadvantaged groups in authoritarian regimes face immense dif-
ficulties  in  gaining  a  role  in  formal  decision-making  processes  and
governance institutions under the non-participatory policymaking pro-
cess.  Consequently,  the  interests  and  voices  of  vulnerable  groups  are
rarely  considered,  which  may  lead  to  social  catastrophe  [94].  For
example, empirical studies conducted in China, a typical authoritarian
regime,  have  revealed  the  tremendous  social  injustice  created  by  the
ongoing  low-carbon  transition  [87,94–97].  Studies  manifest  that  the
rapid  energy  transition  in  China,  driven  by  powerful  political-
administrative interventions, has resulted in serious socio-economic is-
sues, including unemployment, a decline of local tax revenues, and so-
cial unrest [96,97]. Lo [97] points out that rather than creating benefits,
energy transition in extractive areas leads to severe social injustice due
to  the  lack  of  policies  to  alleviate  negative  impacts  from  the  central
government. Scholars also stress that social injustices in authoritarian
regimes are rooted in the imbalance of structural power between state-

owned  and  private  enterprises  [94].  Whereas  many  privately-owned
firms were forced to shut down by China’s energy transition policies,
state-owned enterprises survived owing to their political influence and
connection  [94,97].  Furthermore,  unlike  the  workers  in  state-owned
enterprises who had better access to government support, the majority
of workers in private companies could not receive proper compensation
either from the government or the company [94].

Scholars therefore suggest that it is urgent for authoritarian regimes
such as China to incorporate just transition measures to achieve envi-
ronmental  goals  without  placing  an  unfair  burden  on  disadvantaged
regions, communities and individuals [96–99]. The suggestions tend to
focus on the concept of restorative justice, rather than the more politi-
cally  controversial  concept  of  procedural  justice.  For  instance,  Zhang
and Wang suggest that the central and local governments should intro-
duce targeted policies and measures to assist the regions and industries
that are most affected [98]. Xu proposes that compensation policies be
established to mitigate negative impacts on traditional energy industries
during an energy transition [99].

In sum, the literature of just transition as governance strategies re-
veals how (un)just transition is shaped by political context. The debate
over governance measures with regard to democratic and authoritarian
systems  highlights  an  important  question:  how  to  resolve  the  tension
between  the  urgency  of  climate  action  and  the  inclusion  of  actors  in
decision-making?  Transitioning  away  from  fossil  fuels  is  urgently
needed  because  of  climate  change,  but  it  is  also  important  to  leave
sufficient time for affected industries, communities, and individuals to
rebuild  industries,  institutions,  infrastructure,  and  human  capital  to
tackle  the  profound  social-technical  changes  associated  with  such
transitions  [93].  However,  many  just  transition  debates  focus  on  the
process and the need to include all voices, without a sense of how to
square the need for inclusion and deliberation with the necessary speed
and  acceleration  to  prevent  further  injustice  associated  with  climate
change  [100,101].  In  this  sense,  the  policy  efficiency  offered  by
authoritarianism could be appealing, although the implication for social
justice is highly concerning. Finding a middle ground between proce-
dural justice and urgency concerns could be a challenging task for the-
orists and practitioners of just transition.

6. Just transition as public perception

Studying public perception and attitudes towards low-carbon energy
transition  is  important  public  support  and  legitimacy  is  vital  for
renewable energy projects and policies to be deployed and implemented
smoothly [102]. In addition, social injustices arising from low-carbon
transitions can be detected from public opinions, especially those who
are directly affected [103]. This final theme in the literature defines just
transition  from  the  perspective  of  public  perception,  acceptance,  and
support of transitions to low-carbon energy production.

A  significant  body  of  literature  focuses  on  the  public  perceptions
toward renewable energy in general [104–106] or specific to a certain
type of low-carbon energy such as wind [102,107], solar [108–110], and
nuclear [111–115]. Given that renewable energy is typically framed as a
measure for addressing environmental and climate matters, studies have
examined the relationship between environmental beliefs and attitudes
towards renewable energy [103]. For example, Motz applies a hybrid
discrete choice model with latent variables on stated preference data to
study the preferences of households with respect to energy sources for
electricity generation. The findings indicate that a higher environmental
concern  is  associated  with  a  strong  preference  for  a  renewable-based
energy  mix  [116].  In  contrast,  a  study  that  analyzes  how  residents
living next to wind energy facilities form their attitudes towards energy
transitions  found  that environmental  beliefs play  a  very  weak  role in
influencing individual’  views on renewable energy [103]. Rather, the
distance of their residence from wind turbines is closely related to the
level of support for renewable energy. The authors thus suggested that
policymakers should pay more attention to locally relevant social values

rather than simply framing renewable energy as an environmental issue
to gain wider public support on renewable energy development at the
local level [103].

Transitioning away from fossil fuel production is one of the central
challenges  facing  coal-dependent  communities.  Understanding  how
these communities respond to ongoing or impending low-carbon energy
transitions  is  thus  important,  especially  as  social  opposition  would
impact  the  scale  and  pace  of  energy  transitions  [103].  Thus,  public
perception, acceptance, and support of low-carbon transitions in com-
munities  that  depend  on  fossil  fuels  have  been  closely  examined  by
scholars [27,36,117–120]. For instance, Cha found that mine closures in
the Powder River Basin region in the United States did not change op-
position to energy transition or increase support for a just transition due
to the longstanding importance of coal to the region [119]. Furthermore,
an empirical study regarding the level of support for energy transition
among citizens was conducted in Germany. The study revealed that the
level of unemployment adversely affects respondents’ attitudes towards
energy transition. As such, it is necessary to provide fair compensation to
the victims of energy transition as an important trust-building measure
between citizens and government [121].

To simultaneously achieve a just transition and address the problem
of unemployment in the coal industry, the development of renewable
energy has become a key measure [36]. Studies have argued that it is
important to facilitate acceptance of renewable energy by residents in
fossil fuels producing regions. Hazboun conducted semi-structured in-
terviews  with  community  representatives  in  two  energy  production
areas in the state of Utah. Her study found that residents generally held
negative attitudes toward renewable energy developments within their
communities because they felt that efforts to transition away from fossil
fuels unjustly punished the industries and communities in which they
operated  [36].  Other  scholars  have  compared  public  perceptions  of
different  energy  sources  rather  than  evaluating  public  perceptions  or
acceptance  of  a  single  source  of  energy.  For  example,  Crowe  and  Li
compared public perceptions and acceptance of coal, solar energy, and
energy transition policies in Illinois, Texas, and Vermont. They found
that  a  community’s  sense  of  place,  rooted  in  place  attachment,  land-
scape memory, and land use history should be carefully considered in
such studies [120]. Scholars have also acknowledged that adaptability
in the face of economic vitality is a crucial factor in individuals’  and
communities’  attitudes  toward  energy  transition  [117].  Procedural
fairness is also important in enhancing community involvement, which
plays  a  significant  role  in  reducing  tensions  and  facilitating  more
effective deliberation mechanisms that may gain support from the public
[122].

In  summary,  this  final  theme  in  the  literature  is  predominantly
concerned with stakeholders’ attitudes toward energy transition. These
studies  generally  examine  changes  in  stakeholders’  attitude  toward
energy transition over time, or under specific conditions, explore how
fossil fuel industry stakeholders adapt to energy transition efforts, and
compare public and stakeholder perceptions across different regions and
industries.  This  body  of  literature  discovered  that  individuals’  and
communities’ attitudes towards energy transitions could be influenced
by  a  rich  variety  of  factors  such  as  the  level  of  unemployment,  envi-
ronmental  sensitivity,  and  an  ingrained  connection  to  the  fossil  fuel
industry  [57,116,121].  However,  the  role  of  public  attitudes  in  clean
energy transitions is unclear and worthy of more in-depth investigation.

7. Discussion

Several  interesting  insights  can  be  garnered  from  analyzing  the
different trends in just transition literature. First, there are differences in
understanding the causes of injustice. While there is a shared attempt to
identify vulnerable groups in low-carbon transitions, different trends in
the  literature  exhibit  different  understandings  of  who  should  be
considered vulnerable. The labor-oriented concept was criticized as it
focuses too much on the male-dominated fossil fuel workers, while other

EnergyResearch&SocialScience82(2021)1022917X. Wang and K. Lo

affected groups were largely ignored and targeted. Consequently, just
transition strategies mainly focus on unemployment compensation and
skills training for obtaining new jobs [14,38]. Cha et al. criticize this
approach by stating that a just transition must not just be compensatory
but comprehensive [14]. In contrast, scholars adopting just transition as
a  holistic  framework  of  justice  see  the  groups  most  affected  by  low-
carbon  transition  as  low-income  households,  people  with  health  con-
cerns,  and  residents  living  in  rural  areas  [9].  By  adopting  a  more
transformative  notion  of  justice,  these  scholars  seek  to  address  the
causes of injustice that are rooted in race, class, and gender. As such, the
question  of  just  transition  for  “whom”  is  still  under  debate.  From  a
governance perspective, the problem of injustice is typically attributed
to governance and political systems. This is particularly relevant to the
governance  of  utility-scale  energy  systems  because  of  the  common
phenomenon of lack of transparency and of public participation in the
decision-making process [9]. However, it should be noted that injustices
not only exist in large-scale, centralized energy projects, but decentral-
ized forms of energy can also generate the unfair distribution of benefits
and harms. It should also be noted that these different interpretations of
causes are not mutually exclusive, but rather, are interrelated.

Second,  and  relatedly,  we  note  the  tension  between  different  un-
derstandings of just transition. While the labor-focused concept of just
transition has strong connections to environmental justice and climate
justice  [19,40,42],  there  are  nevertheless  tensions  between  these
different concepts of just transition. For example, Kalt points out that
labor movements and climate movements hold inherently opposite po-
sitions on jobs and the environment as climate movements seek to leave
fossil fuels in the ground, whereas labor unions oppose phasing out fossil
fuels in order to protect high-paying jobs for fossil fuel workers [39]. The
inability to resolve the conflicts between environmental protection and
maintaining fossil fuel-based jobs means that, eventually, the concept of
just transition turns to a “fancy name for a funeral” and becomes allied
to fossil fuel capitalism [38]. In addition, the tension is also mirrored
within the diversity of labor unions or even the contradictory meanings
of just transition given the different levels of utilization of strategy for
just  transition  by  international  labor  unions  within  a  wide  range  of
political contexts around the world [38].

Third,  in  general,  sophisticated  empirical  studies  are  lacking  as
scholars  seem  more  interested  in  creating  various  just  transition
analytical frameworks than in applying these frameworks to empirical
investigations. The focus is often on what should happen or who should
be included in the process, rather than on the efficacy and problems of
just transition frameworks in action, or what was it about transitions to
date  that  made  them  unjust.  Further,  little  is  written  on  the  ways  in
which the just transition framework is coopted or limited to prioritize
the interests of certain strategies, interest groups, and industries. The
literature  would  benefit  from  research  that  examines  how  competing
ideas of just transition are handled in real-world settings, as well as how
governance strategies evolve as just transitions gather momentum.

Fourth, future studies should pay more attention to the power dy-
namics  in  just  transition.  Several  scholars  assert  that  multiple  actors
from government, international institutions, business, and civil society
play  an  essential  role  in  mobilizing  low-carbon  transition  strategies.
However,  critical  examinations  of  the  power  relations  among  these
stakeholders are largely absent from the just transition literature. Future
studies should examine how just transitions are discursively constructed
to form coalitions across a variety of actors, should determine how ac-
tors are strategically mobilized, and should investigate how power re-
lations change (or are  maintained) between incumbent  and emergent
actors over time. The underlying principle of just transition is equity,
which aims at both empowering the weak and weakening the powerful
at the most general level of social justice. However, the concept of just
transition  could  be  co-opted  by  powerful  actors  to  prioritize  certain
strategies, social groups, and industries. The extent to which just tran-
sitions  can  achieve  a  radical  transformation  in  power  relations  is
unclear.

Fifth, given that political contexts play an important role in shaping
the power dynamic and governance of just transition, further research is
needed to explore the relationship between different political systems
and different types and outcomes of just energy transition. It would be
beneficial, for instance, to take a comparative approach that uncovers a
range of similarities and differences between energy transitions taking
place in democratic and authoritarian regimes.

Sixth, future studies should expand their geographic scope to better
understand  how  transitions  unfold  across  different  geographical  con-
texts and why the success and implementation of justice-oriented tran-
sition policies are context-dependent. Just transition research focuses on
the  labor–environment  nexus.  However,  the  suggested  harmonious
relation  between  the  two  is  often  based  on  a  well-developed  “green
economy.” Just transition studies mainly focus on a few industrialized
countries  such  as  the  United  States,  Germany,  United  Kingdom,  and
Australia [27,36,117–120,123]. It would be useful for the literature to
include an examination of the concept and application of just transitions
in developing countries, as addressing justice issues in these countries
poses an even greater challenge, particularly because regions or coun-
tries dominated by mineral and fossil fuel resources face difficulties in
achieving  socio-economic  development  and  ecological  goals  simulta-
neously [124]. This has been proven in the few studies that focus on just
transition in developing countries; for example, Swilling et al. conclude
that a just transition in South Africa is currently unlikely because the
country’s political economy has been dominated by mineral and fossil
fuel  extraction  and  the  political  elites  have  committed  serious  rent-
seeking  and  corrupt  practices  [91].  The  key  challenge  for  scholars  is
thus to incorporate the spatial dimension into an understanding of just
transition, which could stimulate theoretical innovation and refinement.
Moreover, just transition practices in different parts of the world may
shape how a just transition should be pursued at a global level [23]. As

global level, low-carbon energy transitions are a spatially uneven pro-

key challenge for future work on just transitions.

8. Conclusion

To consolidate knowledge, advance the debate over just transition
and our understanding of it, this review focuses on how this term with a
distinctive  historical  backdrop  has  evolved  and  been  adopted  in  aca-
demic circles. Five themes have been identified. First, scholars focusing
on  the  historical  roots  of  just  transition  understand  the  concept  pri-
marily  as  a  labor-oriented  construct  that  links  energy  transition  with
economic transition. Second, some scholars, recognizing the inherit link
between  just  transition  and  other  justice  concepts,  particularly  envi-
ronmental  justice,  climate  justice,  and  energy  justice,  envision  just
transition as an integrated framework of justice. Third, there are some
attempts to develop just transition as a framework for cooperating jus-
tice principles into socio-technical transition studies, thereby bringing
attention  to  the  material  dimensions  of  just  transition.  Fourth,  just
transition can be understood as a governance phenomenon, and scholars
taking this approach focus on how just transition unfolds under different
governance regimes. Fifth, inspired by the idea that justices or injustices
are rooted in “the meaning of life” [53], scholars in this theme explore
the  public  perceptions  and  attitudes  surrounding  the  justice  of  low-
carbon  transitions.  The  review  shows  that,  given  the  proliferation  of
just transition research in recent years, the term has become extended,
multifaceted,  and  to  some  degree  problematically  polysemic,  which
leaves  room  for  confusion  in  interpretation.  It  is  thus  important  to
conceptually distinguish the different strands of just transition, as well
as to recognize the tension between the different strands.

This paper contributes to the literature and practice in several ways.
First,  it  serves  as  a  toolkit  for  researchers  and  students  in  need  of

EnergyResearch&SocialScience82(2021)1022918X. Wang and K. Lo

comprehensive insight into the concept of just transition. Second, given
that the concept is still very much in a development phase, this is an
opportune time to further the debate on just transition and to expand its
scope and relevance as a research field. This paper aims to advance a
research agenda for just transition that can help to illuminate pathways
forward for a more just future. Third, justice transition strategies have
been practiced in many different regions and countries around the world
and these experiences serve as lessons for policymakers. The articulation
of  just  transition  principles  and  strategies  could  have  an  impact  by
making policymakers, who often have too little regard for the long-term
socioeconomic consequences, understand the core values underpinning
the concept [63]. Fourth, this paper may prove to be useful to practi-
tioners and activists interested in social justice, including labor unions,
environmentalists, and climate justice activists. The review shows that
the  synergies  among  these  different  groups,  as  well  as  their  common
civil rights roots, indicate that the concept of just transition may create
opportunities to unite these groups to overturn injustices associated with
low-carbon energy transition.
